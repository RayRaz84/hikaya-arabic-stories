# Narrative Quality Standards (NQS)

Independent story-writing and evaluation framework. Version 1.0, for second-language reading materials.

## 1. Core principle

**Every instructional story must succeed as a story independently of its language-teaching objectives.**

A story should be interesting, coherent, believable within its fictional world, and satisfying to read, even when translated into the learner's first language.

The reader should experience a narrative, not a vocabulary exercise disguised as one.

Language-learning requirements must support the narrative rather than dictate unnatural events, dialogue, or endings.

## 2. The eight narrative quality standards

### NQS-1: Clear character motivation

Every central character must have a recognizable desire, need, question, or purpose.

- The reader understands what the main character wants.
- The character's actions follow logically from that desire.
- Supporting characters have understandable reasons for their behavior.
- Characters do not exist merely to deliver vocabulary or trigger repetitive interactions.
- Motivations can be simple but must be coherent.

**Failure example:** Three characters refuse to give the protagonist an object until unrelated objects are retrieved, without any understandable reason.

**Success standard:** The reader can explain why the characters behave as they do.

### NQS-2: Logical cause and effect

Events must connect through meaningful causal relationships rather than arbitrary sequencing.

- Each important event follows logically from earlier events.
- Problems and obstacles arise from the story situation.
- Characters respond meaningfully to what happens.
- Essential actions are not omitted.
- Solutions follow from information, choices, or circumstances established in the narrative.
- Playful, absurd, or fantastical events are acceptable when consistent with the story's established world.

**Success standard:** The reader understands not only what happens next, but why it happens.

### NQS-3: Narrative movement and progression

A story must develop rather than simply repeat a situation.

- Establish an initial situation.
- Introduce a desire, problem, mystery, or meaningful event.
- Develop the situation through actions, discoveries, obstacles, or interactions.
- Move toward a meaningful outcome.
- Avoid unnecessary detours that do not advance the story, reveal character, or create worthwhile anticipation.

A problem–attempt–solution structure is often useful, but it is not mandatory. Stories may also be organized around discovery, humor, friendship, surprise, misunderstanding, or a simple shared experience.

**Success standard:** Something meaningful changes between the beginning and the end.

### NQS-4: Reader engagement and curiosity

The narrative should give readers a reason to continue. Possible sources of engagement: curiosity about what happens next, a relatable desire or problem, anticipation and prediction, humor, a surprising discovery, an interesting character relationship, emotional involvement, a playful misunderstanding.

Not every story needs suspense, conflict, or an unexpected twist.

**Success standard:** The reader has a genuine reason to want the next sentence, beyond completing a learning activity.

### NQS-5: Natural characters and dialogue

Characters should behave like participants in a story rather than instructional devices.

- Dialogue has a believable communicative purpose.
- Characters respond to what others actually say.
- Requests, reactions, and decisions fit the situation.
- Emotions and reactions are appropriate to the events.
- Dialogue reveals intentions, relationships, or useful information.
- Avoid repetitive exchanges that sound mechanical or artificially constructed.

Repetition is acceptable when it creates humor, anticipation, or a meaningful recurring pattern.

**Success standard:** The dialogue would make sense as an actual interaction within the story's world.

### NQS-6: Narrative economy, clarity, and flow

Every sentence should contribute something worthwhile: advance the action, clarify the situation, develop a character, build anticipation, establish a detail needed later, create atmosphere or humor, or reinforce a meaningful narrative pattern.

- Avoid unnecessary descriptions and redundant explanations.
- Maintain clear references to people, objects, and events.
- Use logical transitions.
- Avoid unexplained shifts in location, time, or perspective.
- Preserve a natural rhythm between narration, action, and dialogue.
- Do not sacrifice logical meaning merely to repeat vocabulary.

**Success standard:** The story is easy to follow and contains no significant confusion or unnecessary narrative padding.

### NQS-7: Setup, development, and payoff

Important narrative details should serve a purpose.

- Establish important objects, characters, and circumstances before their decisive use when appropriate.
- Allow earlier details to become relevant later.
- Make discoveries and surprises understandable in retrospect.
- Avoid resolving major problems through completely unprepared events.
- Avoid inserting characters or objects that have no meaningful narrative function.

An early detail may return later as a humorous or satisfying payoff. Not every detail needs a payoff, but significant details should not feel pointless.

**Success standard:** The ending or resolution rewards attention to earlier parts of the story.

### NQS-8: Meaningful and satisfying ending

The ending must be evaluated as a central feature, not an afterthought.

- Address the main desire, problem, or narrative expectation.
- Show a meaningful outcome or change.
- Follow naturally from the events that preceded it.
- Provide an appropriate emotional or narrative payoff.
- Avoid adding a surprise solely for novelty.
- Avoid arbitrary reversals that undermine the resolution.
- Avoid closing with a sentence inserted only to recycle vocabulary.

Possible successful endings: the problem is resolved; characters cooperate; a misunderstanding becomes clear; a character learns or discovers something; an unexpected event produces meaningful humor; a relationship develops; the character makes a small but important choice.

A happy ending is not mandatory. The resolution must fit the story's intended effect.

**Success standard:** The reader finishes with a sense of satisfaction, amusement, discovery, or meaningful anticipation.

## 3. Mandatory narrative integrity tests

Every story must pass all five tests before publication. A failure in any test requires revision, regardless of the story's numerical score.

| Test | Question |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Native-language test | If the story were translated into the reader's first language, would it still be worth reading? |
| 2. Motivation test | Can we clearly explain why the main characters act as they do? |
| 3. Causality test | Can we explain how each important event leads to the next without inventing missing information? |
| 4. Narrative progression test | Does the situation meaningfully develop, or does the story merely repeat the same exchange? |
| 5. Ending test | Does the ending fulfill, transform, or deliberately and meaningfully challenge the expectation established earlier? |

## 4. Scoring rubric

Score each category from 0 to 5. Final score = sum of each category's weight × (its score ÷ 5).

| Criterion | Weight (%) |
| --- | ---: |
| Logical cause and effect | 20 |
| Ending and emotional payoff | 20 |
| Character motivation | 15 |
| Narrative progression | 15 |
| Reader engagement | 10 |
| Natural dialogue and behavior | 10 |
| Clarity, economy, and flow | 5 |
| Setup and payoff | 5 |

Anchors: 0 Absent · 1 Poor (major problems consistently undermine the story) · 2 Weak (significant deficiencies) · 3 Adequate (functional, improvements needed) · 4 Strong (effective, coherent, engaging) · 5 Excellent (particularly natural, memorable, or satisfying for the audience and length).

| Total | Verdict |
| --- | --- |
| 90–100 | Excellent narrative quality |
| 85–89 | Strong; suitable for learner testing |
| 70–84 | Promising, but revision required |
| Below 70 | Substantial narrative redevelopment required |

Approval requires: (1) a total of at least 85; (2) all five integrity tests passed; (3) at least 4/5 for character motivation, causal coherence, and ending quality; (4) no unresolved major narrative inconsistencies.

These thresholds are internal editorial standards, not scientifically validated measures of reader enjoyment.

## 5. Automatic revision triggers

Flag a story for revision when:

- A character behaves primarily to create an artificial vocabulary repetition.
- An important obstacle has no understandable cause.
- Dialogue is repetitive without narrative or communicative purpose.
- A major action is missing from the causal sequence.
- The same situation repeats without development or meaningful variation.
- The story introduces a detail that appears important but never becomes relevant.
- The ending contradicts or undermines the established narrative without a worthwhile purpose.
- A surprise feels random rather than earned.
- The final sentence exists mainly to repeat a target structure.
- The story resolves the immediate problem but leaves the reader without a meaningful sense of completion.
- Removing the instructional repetitions reveals that almost no narrative remains.

These are diagnostic flags, not automatic rejection rules. A deliberate artistic choice may justify an apparent exception.

## 6. Narrative development workflow

1. **Establish the story:** main character, central desire or situation, main obstacle or source of interest, supporting characters, meaningful developments, intended resolution, desired reader response.
2. **Develop the narrative logic:** check that each major event follows from what precedes it; remove arbitrary obstacles, unexplained exchanges and unnecessary events.
3. **Design the ending:** what did the reader expect, what has changed, why is it satisfying, does it connect to something established earlier? Don't choose a surprising ending over a simple, satisfying one by default.
4. **Write and refine** with natural pacing, purposeful dialogue, clear behavior and coherent progression. Don't add sentences solely for repetition targets.
5. **Independent evaluation** against the eight standards, the rubric and the integrity tests. Identify weaknesses before changing anything.
6. **Revise** the actual weaknesses with the simplest changes that genuinely strengthen the narrative; don't just add words to look richer.
7. **Language-level adaptation:** adapt the language to the learner's vocabulary and structures while preserving causal logic, motivations and resolution. If language limits damage the narrative, revise the plot using familiar language.
8. **Final reader-experience review:** read it as a story; test with actual learners when possible, without assuming a score proves engagement.

## 7. Creative flexibility

Do not require every story to contain exactly one problem, exactly two failed attempts, an unexpected twist, a moral lesson, a character transformation, a fixed number of characters, a complicated conflict, or the same structure. A short, charming story about two characters playing together may be more effective than a complicated problem-solving narrative.

**Narrative quality depends on coherence, interest, and satisfying execution, not structural complexity.**

## 8. Final governing rule

**Never approve a weak narrative simply because it meets the language-learning requirements.** Narrative quality and instructional quality are independent requirements; a story must pass both. The goal is stories that learners understand, enjoy, remember, and genuinely want to continue reading.
